## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The AI–IAM landscape

- Artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning (ML) and large language models (LLMs), is rapidly being integrated into the IAM workflow.
- This brings new opportunities to accelerate climate policy research.
- It also brings new challenges for scientific transparency, verification, data integrity and bias, as well as the relationship between science, policy and broader society.

### 1.2 Purpose of these guidelines

- Establish community-led standards of practice.
- Support researchers in deciding whether and how to use AI, while safeguarding scientific credibility.
- Secure the reliability of research that informs global climate and sustainability policy.

### 1.3 Key principles

- **Reliability:** ensure the physical and economic consistency of AI-supported workflows.
- **Transparency:** disclose methods, training inputs and prompts clearly.
- **Respect:** credit and protect others' work, drafts and intellectual property.
- **Accountability:** maintain expert human oversight of, and responsibility for, all scientific, computational and text outputs.

## 2. Guidelines on the Responsible Use of AI in IAM Research

### 2.1 Expert human accountability and content verification

- **Maintain human accountability.** Remain fully responsible for the scientific integrity of all research outputs, whatever AI tools are used. Do not list AI tools or LLMs as authors or co-authors: they cannot take responsibility for the work.
- **Verify facts, citations and summaries.** When using generative AI for literature reviews or manuscript drafting, manually verify every cited source and fact-check generated claims. Remove fabricated or hallucinated citations, so that every source is authentic and accurately represented.
- **Cross-validate automated analyses.** Systematically cross-validate AI outputs used in data analysis and modeling. Verify that AI-generated and emulator-derived outputs adhere to fundamental physical laws and established economic principles.

### 2.2 The AI-generated scenario standard

*AI-generated scenarios* are scenarios generated by an AI system, without an IAM, that mirror the format of *human-modeled scenarios*: scenarios modeled by human researchers with a conventional IAM that has an explicit model structure. Once in a database, the two are hard to tell apart.

- **Label and document AI-generated scenarios.** Label them clearly in all datasets, presentations, publications, policy briefs and press releases. The label should be prominent, explicit and accessible to third-party users. Every AI-generated scenario must carry the metadata flag `AI-generated = true`.
- **Apply human-in-the-loop validation.** Use domain expertise to validate that AI-generated scenarios reflect plausible real-world dynamics and conform to fundamental physical and economic laws.
- **Keep AI-generated scenarios separate.** Maintain a strict, unambiguous distinction between AI-generated scenarios and human-modeled scenarios in all databases, presentations and publications.

### 2.3 Transparency, disclosure and reproducibility

- **Disclose methods.** Disclose AI use fully in the methods or supplementary materials of a publication: tool name, exact model version, provider and how the tool was integrated.
- **Ensure reproducibility of stochastic tools.** When using stochastic generative AI tools, document and share system prompts, random seeds and sampling settings.
- **Mitigate hidden prompts and biases.** Be aware that commercial AI interfaces contain pre-configured, system-level instructions hidden from the user. Identify and mitigate the biases or hidden assumptions these introduce, to safeguard scientific objectivity.

### 2.4 Intellectual property and collaborative consent

- **Protect intellectual property and confidentiality.** Do not enter others' unpublished research, draft manuscripts, custom model code or confidential scenario data into public or external generative AI tools where data security cannot be guaranteed. Safeguard pre-publication work within the research community.
- **Credit training sources.** Cite the scenario databases and modeling teams whose scenarios were used to train an AI tool or emulator.
- **Obtain consent in shared workspaces.** Obtain explicit consent from all human participants before using AI tools to record, transcribe or summarize meetings and workshops.

### 2.5 Environmental footprint of AI computation

- **Acknowledge and monitor environmental impacts.** Recognize the substantial energy use and environmental footprint of training complex AI models and of high-resolution data, and align research methods with environmental sustainability principles.
- **Disclose and mitigate carbon footprints.** Monitor and disclose the estimated computational and carbon footprint of highly intensive AI workflows. Consider whether a smaller tool suffices, and prefer energy-efficient or clean-energy computing.
